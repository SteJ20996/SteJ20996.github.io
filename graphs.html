01 · The Skill

Reading and making pictures of math

Tables, charts and geometry figures pack a lot of math into a small space. Reading them, drawing them, and noticing when one misleads are skills of their own. (The skill is called graphicacy.)

Each kind hides easy slips: a bar whose height is not its count, a figure that only looks like it has a right angle, a picture that doubles in width but grows four times in area.

02 · What Delta Looks At

Four things, all at once

  • Every number you placeIs each one in the right row and column?
  • How a picture holds numbersHeight, area, length and position, and which one carries the count.
  • What a figure really tells youWhat is given, and what only looks true.
  • How sure you wereSet next to how you actually did.

03 · The Honest Part

Simple rules, and we say so

The checking here is done by simple rules. Numbers, choices and points on a grid are checked exactly. Table labels are matched against a word list, in English or 中文, so any sensible layout counts.

What you write in your own words is read by a keyword rule, which cannot read everything a person might say. In the next phase, an AI model reads your labels and your words the way a teacher would.

      05 · Two Directions

      Words to a table, then a table to your own reasoning

      ¶Wordsa passage with numbers in it
      A · Build itslips: “fewer than”, percents, totals
      ▦Tablein your own layout
      B · Read itslips: counts or shares, combined groups
      ✎Your reasoningwhat it shows, in your words
      Each slip sends you to a short task aimed at that slip. Every step ends in the mirror: how sure you felt, next to how right you were.

      These are two different skills. Going one way can be much harder than going back, so each gets its own practice.

      06 · Where The Tricky Parts Come From

      Each trap is a slip people commonly make

      • "Fewer than" read as take awayThe sentence says fewer, but the number you need is the bigger one.
      • A percent written as a count35% of 80 books is 28 books, not 35.
      • Comparing counts, not sharesA bigger group can have more of something and still a smaller share.
      • Trusting the combined numbersPutting groups together can flip a comparison that holds inside each group.
      • A bar's height read as its countWhen bars have different widths, the area shows how many.
      • A long piece of a box plot read as moreEach of the four pieces holds about a quarter of the data.
      • An axis that does not start at zeroA small gap can be drawn to look huge.
      • Counting the squares in one viewOne square in a view can hide a whole column of cubes.
      • Trusting how a figure looksOnly what is given counts, not what the drawing suggests.
      • Area scaled like lengthDouble every length and the area grows four times.

      Background: Duval (2006) on moving between ways of showing math; Lewis & Mayer (1987); Hegarty, Mayer & Monk (1995); Parker & Leinhardt (1995); Martí et al. (2011); Friel, Curcio & Bright (2001); Simpson (1951); Cooper & Shore (2008) and Kaplan et al. (2014) on histograms; Lem et al. (2013) on box plots; Pandey et al. (2015) on misleading charts; Ben-Chaim, Lappan & Houang (1988) on views of solids; Duval (1995) and Hershkowitz (1989) on reading geometry figures; De Bock et al. (2002) on scaling area like length.
