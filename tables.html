01 · The Skill

Moving numbers between words and tables

A lot of real math starts as a paragraph: a report, a news story, a word problem. Before you can compare anything, you have to pull the numbers out and put each one where it belongs.

The reverse is just as real. A table holds the facts, but you still have to say what they mean. Both steps are easy to get wrong without noticing.

02 · What Delta Looks At

Four things, all at once

  • Every number you placeIs each one in the right row and column?
  • The tricky sentences"Fewer than", a percent, a total that has to be split.
  • How you read a tableCounts or shares, one group or all of them combined.
  • How sure you wereSet next to how you actually did.

03 · The Honest Part

Simple rules, and we say so

The checking here is done by simple rules. They match your table labels against a word list, in English or 中文, and compare every number. Any sensible layout counts: groups down the side, groups across the top, or one long list.

What you write about a table is read by a keyword rule too. Neither rule can read everything a person might say. In the next phase, an AI model reads your labels and your words the way a teacher would.

      05 · Two Directions

      Words to a table, then a table to your own reasoning

      ¶Wordsa passage with numbers in it
      A · Build itslips: “fewer than”, percents, totals
      ▦Tablein your own layout
      B · Read itslips: counts or shares, combined groups
      ✎Your reasoningwhat it shows, in your words
      Each slip sends you to a short task aimed at that slip. Every step ends in the mirror: how sure you felt, next to how right you were.

      These are two different skills. Going one way can be much harder than going back, so each gets its own practice.

      06 · Where The Tricky Parts Come From

      Each trap is a slip people commonly make

      • "Fewer than" read as take awayThe sentence says fewer, but the number you need is the bigger one, so you add.
      • Copying a number from the sentenceThe number in the sentence is a difference, not the count.
      • A percent written as a count35% of 80 books is 28 books, not 35.
      • A total used as a partA group's total goes in a total cell, not in one of its parts.
      • Comparing counts, not sharesA bigger group can have more of something and still a smaller share of it.
      • Trusting the combined numbersPutting groups together can flip a comparison that holds inside every group.

      Background: Duval (2006) on moving between ways of showing math; Lewis & Mayer (1987) on "fewer than" wording; Hegarty, Mayer & Monk (1995) on copying numbers from the text; Parker & Leinhardt (1995) on percents; Martí et al. (2011) on building two-way tables; Friel, Curcio & Bright (2001) on levels of reading a graph or table; Simpson (1951) on combined groups.
