01 · The Skill

Pulling numbers out of words

A lot of real math starts as a paragraph: a report, a news story, a word problem. Before you can compare anything, you have to pull the numbers out and put each one where it belongs. That step is a skill of its own. It is also easy to get wrong without noticing.

02 · What Delta Looks At

Four things, all at once

  • Every numberIs each one in the right row and column?
  • The tricky sentences"Fewer than", a percent, a total that has to be split.
  • How you built itA grid or a list, which labels you used, whether your totals add up.
  • How sure you wereSet next to how you actually did.

03 · The Honest Part

Simple rules, and we say so

The checking here is done by simple rules. They match your labels against a word list, in English or 中文, and compare every number. Any sensible layout counts: groups down the side, groups across the top, or one long list.

The rules cannot read an unusual label that is not on the list. In the next phase, an AI model reads your labels the way a teacher would.

04 · Live Demo

Table Lab

You get one passage. Build a table that holds its numbers, in whatever shape makes sense to you. Say how sure you are. You get no feedback until the end.

Δ  Table Lab
Step 1 of 3 · Read, then build your table

Put labels in the top row and the first column, and numbers in the other cells. Add or remove rows and columns as you need. There is no single right shape.

Step 2 of 3 · How sure are you that every number in your table is in the right place?

Scoring works like this: a confident right table earns the most, and a confident wrong one costs the most. So honesty is your best strategy. (The method is called certainty-based marking.)

    05 · Two Directions

    Words to a table, then a table to your own reasoning

    • HERE NOWWords to a tableYou turn a passage into a table. The hard part is putting each number where it belongs.
    • NEXTA table to your own reasoningYou start from a table that is not so simple and write what it shows, in your own words. Delta checks whether each claim really follows from the numbers.

    These are two different skills. Going one way can be much harder than going back, so each gets its own practice.

    06 · Where The Tricky Parts Come From

    Each trap is a slip students commonly make

    • "Fewer than" read as take awayThe sentence says fewer, but the missing number is bigger, so you have to add.
    • Copying a number from the sentenceThe number in the sentence is a difference, not the count.
    • A percent written as a count35% of 80 books is 28 books, not 35.
    • A total used as a partA group's total goes in a total cell, not in one of its parts.

    Background: Duval (2006) on moving between ways of showing math; Lewis & Mayer (1987) on "fewer than" wording; Hegarty, Mayer & Monk (1995) on copying numbers from the text; Parker & Leinhardt (1995) on percents; Martí et al. (2011) on building two-way tables.
